## What happens when blood pressure rises?

Blood pressure measures the force exerted by circulating blood on the walls of your arteries. It is recorded using two key numbers:

- **Systolic Pressure (the top number):** Indicates the pressure in your arteries when your heart contracts and pumps blood.
- **Diastolic Pressure (the bottom number):** Reflects the pressure when your heart relaxes between beats.

Normal blood pressure typically ranges around 120/80 mmHg. When this balance is disrupted and consistently exceeds 130/80 mmHg, your cardiovascular system begins to experience stress. The arteries; designed to be flexible and resilient; gradually become stiffer and narrower due to constant pressure. This reduces their ability to deliver oxygen-rich blood efficiently, forcing your heart to exert more effort.

Over time, this excessive workload may lead to left ventricular hypertrophy (thickening of the heart muscle), increased risk of arterial plaque buildup, and eventual organ strain, particularly in the kidneys where delicate filtration structures are sensitive to pressure changes.

## The connection between hypertension and kidney health

Few people realize how intimately hypertension and kidney health are linked. The kidneys play a crucial role in regulating blood pressure by balancing fluid and sodium levels in the body. When blood pressure is too high, the small blood vessels (glomeruli) inside the kidneys can become damaged, impairing their ability to filter waste effectively.

Conversely, kidney disease itself can raise blood pressure by disrupting hormonal and fluid balance; creating a dangerous cycle known as the hypertension–kidney axis. This interdependence is why patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) often have high blood pressure, and those with uncontrolled hypertension are at elevated risk for kidney decline.

## Types of hypertension: understanding the differences

Not all hypertension is the same. Identifying the correct type helps us design personalized treatment strategies tailored to your body's specific needs.

## 1. primary (essential) hypertension

This is the most common form, accounting for nearly 90–95% of all cases. It develops gradually over many years due to a combination of genetic factors, aging, lifestyle habits, and environmental influences. Common contributors include high sodium intake, obesity, stress, lack of physical activity, and excessive alcohol consumption. While its exact cause may be unclear, primary hypertension is highly manageable through lifestyle adjustments and appropriate medical therapy.

## 2. secondary hypertension

This type results from an underlying medical condition that disrupts the body's natural regulation of blood pressure. Possible causes include:

- Kidney disease or renal artery stenosis
- Hormonal disorders such as hyperthyroidism, Cushing's syndrome, or primary aldosteronism
- Sleep apnea
- Certain medications such as oral contraceptives, steroids, or decongestants
- Adrenal gland tumors (pheochromocytoma)

Unlike primary hypertension, secondary hypertension may improve or even resolve when the root cause is properly treated. At WellGen Health, we specialize in pinpointing these causes through advanced diagnostics and multidisciplinary evaluation.

## Why hypertension is known as "the silent killer"

One of the most challenging aspects of hypertension is that it often produces no obvious symptoms until major organs are already compromised. Many individuals live for years unaware that their blood pressure is high, only discovering it after experiencing a severe event like a stroke, heart attack, or kidney failure.

Subtle signs such as headaches, dizziness, shortness of breath, nosebleeds, or blurred vision may occur, but these are not specific indicators. Regular screening and monitoring are, therefore, the only reliable methods to detect hypertension early.

At WellGen Health, we educate patients about the importance of routine blood pressure checks; whether in the clinic or at home. Early identification allows for timely lifestyle adjustments, medication optimization, and risk reduction before damage becomes irreversible.

## Identifying triggers and risk factors

Hypertension does not develop overnight. It is the cumulative result of genetic predisposition, environmental exposure, and lifestyle patterns. The most common risk factors include:

- Family history of high blood pressure or cardiovascular disease
- High-sodium diet and processed food consumption
- Sedentary lifestyle and obesity
- Chronic stress and poor sleep quality
- Smoking and excessive alcohol intake
- Age-related vascular changes
- Underlying conditions such as diabetes, kidney disease, or thyroid disorders

By recognizing and modifying these risk factors early, you can significantly lower your long-term cardiovascular and renal risks. Our specialists guide you through evidence-based interventions that target both modifiable lifestyle behaviors and underlying metabolic conditions contributing to high blood pressure.
